# Action throughline and shot design

List the events the audience must understand from the original brief, then design shots. For open creative choices, compare possible approaches internally and implement one; do not require multiple proposals for every short film. Preserve user-specified shot order and cut times.

For each shot, identify who does what, which state continues from the previous shot, and what the camera reveals. Camera movement supports visible information rather than disguising inactive characters. Allow time to read key outcomes; deliberate stillness is not a failure.

- **Chase:** make pursuer, target and changing separation clear. Choose lane changes, interception, corners or brief instability as appropriate to the brief. Speed comes from parallax, low angles and relative motion, not constant camera shake over linear sliding.
- **Blocking or hiding a prop:** approach → interception → stop/reaction → prop action → confrontation. Preserve the same prop and readable hand contact. An over-the-shoulder shot must use the correct observer and subject; a distant two-shot cannot replace a requested close-up.
- **Product:** control stationary object and orbiting camera independently. A 360-degree orbit means one complete camera revolution in world space, followed by the requested push-in; rotating the product is not a substitute. Keep the featured part inside the final close-up.
- **Turning or warning:** establish the approaching stimulus before awareness and the head turn. Give a sphere head a nose, facial plane or directional hair cue. In silent films, convey warnings through readable gestures and reactions; do not claim the audience heard exact dialogue.

Add slow motion only when requested or justified by the brief. Define world-time mapping separately from camera playback time; reusing a slowed discrete time for both does not create independently moving photography.

Use silhouette, tonal separation and restrained identification colors. Simplify distant buildings and trees; reserve detail for contacts, feet, wheels and facing cues that affect comprehension. Night scenes still need enough fill to reveal required actions.
